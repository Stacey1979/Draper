Navigation without GPS

Draper builds navigation systems that keep working when satellite signals don't. Our inertial sensors, estimation algorithms, and alternative navigation methods hold position and attitude through jamming, spoofing, deep water, and deep space — environments where GPS is denied, degraded, or was never available.

The signal is the weakest link.

Almost everything that moves now assumes a satellite is telling it where it is. That assumption fails in more places than most systems account for: under water, under a jungle canopy, inside a contested airspace, beneath the surface of another planet. It also fails on purpose, because a signal that can be received can be jammed or imitated.

Draper has worked on this problem since the beginning. The organization's founding work was inertial guidance — determining position from a system's own motion rather than from an outside reference — and that lineage runs directly into the sensors, filters, and flight software we build today.

What has changed is the range of environments. The same underlying question now has to be answered for a submarine, a satellite, a lunar lander, and a hand-carried device, each with different constraints on power, size, and how much error is survivable.

How we approach it

We build the sensor, not just the system

Navigation accuracy is bounded by the physics of the measuring device. Developing inertial sensors in-house — rather than integrating someone else's — means the performance ceiling is ours to move.

Every input is imperfect, so fuse them

No single reference is trustworthy alone. Estimation and sensor fusion combine inertial data with terrain, celestial, magnetic, and signal-of-opportunity references, weighting each by how much it can be believed at that moment.

It has to survive the environment it's for

An algorithm that works in simulation is not a navigation system. Packaging, thermal behavior, radiation tolerance, and qualification testing decide whether the work reaches the field.
